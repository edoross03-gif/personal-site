ABV Case study · Sep 2024 – Mar 2026

Building a finance function for a music startup, then stepping back.

Have you ever tried to forecast cash for a company whose clients are emerging rap artists? One line of the operating manual I wrote says it plainly: artists have a low credit score, so forecast incoming cash prudently.

This is what I built at AB Vision, in the order I built it, with the numbers from the files. Client names, prices, salaries and euro balances stay out. Ratios, models and methods are in.

What it is

Release planning, social strategy, paid media and sound direction for emerging urban artists, sold as monthly retainers in three tiers. Still running: abvision.it Live

Where

A Milan studio space shared with an independent label and a recording-studio group.

Who

Three partners and four operators, plus rotating juniors. Founder and CEO: Alexander Bonzano.

Scale

About 20 artists on quarterly contracts when the business plan was written, Oct 2025. Thesis

My seat

Assistant project manager from Sep 2024, then financial controller and board member until March 2026. We also built the website together.

Board minutes

“Eddy: business plan, accounting and administration.”

SYSTEM 1 · CASH

A pipeline tracker and a cash calendar, two views of the same film

Revenue arrived as quotes and renewals negotiated over WhatsApp. Nobody could say what cash would land, or when.

FPS logs every quote, renewal, draft and potential deal: issue date, due date, days late, notified, replied, next action. An Apps Script pulls every open deal into one list. FDC is a day-by-day cash calendar: confirmed movements in black, forecasts in yellow, moved to actuals within 24 hours of payment.

The routine around it: sheet updates every working day, a one-hour call with the CEO on Monday, Wednesday and Friday, a written recap on Tuesday and Thursday, and a monthly outlook at the start of each month.

Result. Payment delays, inbound and outbound, down by about 30%. CV

SYSTEM 2 · UNIT ECONOMICS

What each client tier really cost to serve

I priced every service line by the hour: rent and running costs plus team pay, spread over the hours actually available. Then I multiplied by the hours each tier consumes per month across media buying, social strategy, sound direction, consulting and project management.

Cost to serve ate between 64% and 71% of list price, depending on the tier. Mid-tier clients were the most profitable; top-tier clients, the ones everyone wanted, were not. Model

Finding. The top tier had the thinnest margin of all in the month a deal closed: 10.9% once the closer's commission was paid. A repricing scenario of +20–25% lifted closing-month margin to 25–30% on every tier.

SYSTEM 3 · CAPACITY

The hour budget: where the team was full and where it was idle

Each service line had an owner and a monthly hour cap. I enumerated every possible roster mix, written like 22-5-2 for entry-mid-top, computed idle capacity per line and intersected the feasible sets across lines, so the answer to “can we take this artist?” became a lookup.

Finding. In the November 2025 plan the team was booked for about 297 of 560 available hours, yet sound direction sat at 100% while influencer marketing and magazine placement sat under 5%. The constraint was one bottleneck, not headcount.

SYSTEM 4 · ROSTER MIX

Same team, different mix: +22%

With hours per tier as constraints and monthly fee as the objective, I solved the roster as a small linear program. Moving from 10 entry, 10 mid and 5 top clients to 30, 5 and 5 raised monthly contribution by 22% under the same caps. Media buying, social strategy and visual direction all ended exactly at their limits.

The model used list price, not margin. The October cost-to-serve work above is what corrected it, and why entry-tier volume only holds up with repricing.

SYSTEM 5 · PLAN, BOARD, PEOPLE

A business plan that became my thesis, and a hiring machine

The business plan: Italian recorded-music market from FIMI data, Porter's five forces, a market of about 30,000 emerging artists narrowing to 5,000–8,000 who buy professional services and 250 reachable in three years; a quarterly cohort model for churn, retention and acquisition; a 15-month P&L, a five-year projection, and a microcredit with a seven-year amortisation schedule to fund growth without dilution. Thesis

On the people side: job specs for four roles, about 100 applications screened across three pipelines, a 72-hour work-sample test (“organise the CEO's Monday”) scored on a weighted rubric, and the operating manual for my own successor in accounting.

END Why I stepped back

AB Vision is still running. I chose to step out of it.

What happened

From January 2026 I was running AB Vision's finance alongside a full-time internship at Kolme. Both deserved full attention, and I couldn't give it to both.

The honest part

I had to choose. Kolme was where I was learning the most, and I'd started building toward a different path: corporate finance, automation, and a master's abroad. A finance function needs someone fully committed to it every day, and that was no longer me. So in March 2026 I stepped back.

What stayed

The company, the website we built together, and the systems above. I wrote an operating manual so whoever took over accounting wouldn't start from zero.

  1. Choose, then commit.

    Two roles that each needed full attention were getting half of mine. Choosing, and stepping back cleanly with a manual for my successor, beat fading out of both.

  2. Cultural fit of the context matters as much as the content.

    We ran a process-driven company inside the Italian urban music scene. The processes worked; getting a market that runs on WhatsApp and gut feel to follow them was the hard part.

  3. Cash is a calendar, not a balance.

    Forecast by day, assume clients pay late, and tell suppliers early when a payment will slip.
